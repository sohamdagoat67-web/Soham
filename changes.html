Exploring Physical & Chemical Changes

1. What is a Change?

In science, a change is the transformation of a substance from one state, form, or composition to another.

Physical Changes

Alters only physical properties (shape, size, state). No new substance is created. Molecules remain identical. These transformations are usually temporary and easy to reverse (e.g., ice melting into water).

Chemical Changes

A reaction occurs to build entirely new substances with distinct chemical properties. Atomic bonds break and rearrange. These are typically permanent and hard to reverse (e.g., unexpected color shifts, gas bubbles, or light emissions).

3. Properties Comparison Table

Property Breakdown Physical Change Chemical Change
Core Definition Alters appearance without altering composition. Reactants convert into new molecular structures.
Reversibility Easy to reverse. Highly difficult or impossible to reverse.
New Materials No brand new substances are generated. Creates one or more new substances.
Common Signs Changes in phase, shape, or total volume. Bubbling gas, light, or unexpected color shifts.
Examples Melting ice, crushing a can, tearing paper. Burning wood, rusting iron, baking a cake.

4. The Curious Case of a Burning Candle

A burning candle is a brilliant example of a process that exhibits both physical and chemical changes simultaneously.

The Physical Change: The heat of the flame melts solid wax into liquid wax. As it flows away, it cools and solidifies back into wax. It changes its physical state, but no new substance forms.

The Chemical Change: Liquid wax travels up the wick and vaporises. This vapour reacts with oxygen in the air (combustion) to create completely new substances: carbon dioxide, water vapour, heat, and soot. The burnt wax cannot be recovered.
